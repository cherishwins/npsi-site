# The Three Doors

Under U.S. defense law, Canada has been domestic since 1992. A Canadian miner's map to American federal money — one door where that counts, and two where it does not.

In November 2022, Canada ordered three Chinese companies out of its lithium juniors. The reasoning was sound; the replacement capital never fully arrived. But one replacement was written into U.S. law in 1992: the Defense Production Act defines *domestic source* to include Canada, and Canadian companies qualify for Title III grants, loans, loan guarantees, and purchase commitments on the same footing as a firm in Ohio. This briefing maps that door and two others that do not depend on it — a Department of Energy pilot program that requires a U.S. site, and an Export-Import Bank program that finances foreign projects on the strength of their American customers — and the navigation moat that stalls applicants.

## 1. The Story We Tell

### Ottawa closed one door and called it strategy.

In November 2022, Canada ordered three Chinese companies out of its lithium juniors. [ISED, Investment Canada Act national security decisions, orders of 2 Nov 2022] The reasoning was sound: China owns the processing layer, and ownership is leverage. But the replacement capital never fully arrived. Projects stalled in the valley of death between discovery and production.

#### C$117.1B

138 major Canadian mining projects under construction or planned, 2024 to 2034. About half of them — 67, worth C$72.4 billion — involve critical minerals. [NRCan, Major Projects Planned or Under Construction 2024–2034, inventory as of September 2024]

## 2. The Fact

### 1992.

The U.S. Defense Production Act defines *domestic source* to include Canada. It has for over three decades. Under Title III, Canadian companies qualify for American defense-industrial grants, loans, loan guarantees, and purchase commitments on the same footing as a firm in Ohio. In December 2023 Congress extended the term to Australia and the United Kingdom, for Title III only and as a fallback: their firms count where U.S. and Canadian firms cannot meet a national-defense need, and the Pentagon must certify that to Congress 30 days in advance. Canadian firms need no such certificate. [50 U.S.C. §4552(7), as amended by Pub. L. 102-558 §132 (1992) and Pub. L. 118-31 §1080 (2023)]

## 3. The New Posture

### Washington stopped writing cheques and started buying shares.

In July 2025 the Pentagon bought US$400 million of convertible preferred stock in a rare-earth producer, with warrants that position it as the largest shareholder; set a ten-year floor of US$110 a kilogram under the company's NdPr output; and committed to take every magnet from a new plant for ten years. The buyer of last resort became a shareholder of first resort. [MP Materials, company announcement and Form 8-K, 10 July 2025]

## 4. Door One — Defense Production Act, Title III

Grants, loans, loan guarantees, and purchase commitments for supply the Pentagon deems critical. This is the door where Canada's domestic-source status does the work: Canadian firms apply as domestic sources, on Canadian ground. This is not theory.

### US$20M

U.S. Department of Defense award to Electra Battery Materials under Title III, 19 August 2024, for the Temiskaming Shores cobalt sulfate refinery. American defense money on Ontario ground. Fortune Minerals and Lomiko Metals took DoD awards before Electra did. [Electra Battery Materials news release, 19 Aug 2024; U.S. DoD, 19 Aug 2024]

## 5. Door Two — Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act §40210 (42 U.S.C. §18743)

Department of Energy grants of up to US$10 million for pilot projects that process, recycle, or develop critical minerals in the United States. Here Canada's domestic-source status does not apply. U.S. siting is a statutory condition, not a scoring preference, so a Canadian mine or refinery is ineligible on its own: a Canadian company uses this door with a U.S. site, or a U.S. partner that has one. Congress authorized US$100 million a year for fiscal 2021 to 2024 only. Structure decides eligibility before merit gets a vote. [42 U.S.C. §18743(d), enacted by Pub. L. 117-58, div. D, §40210]

## 6. Door Three — EXIM's Supply Chain Resiliency Initiative

Export-Import Bank financing for international projects — Canadian ones included — that carry long-term offtake contracts with U.S. companies. Here Canada is a foreign supplier selling to American buyers, and that is the point. EXIM treats the offtake as the test of eligibility and sizes the financing by the share of the project's output committed to U.S. buyers. A signed American customer is not just revenue; it is the application. One date to carry: EXIM's charter runs to 31 December 2026 unless Congress renews it. [Export-Import Bank of the United States, Supply Chain Resiliency Initiative; 12 U.S.C. §635f]

## 7. The Turn

The gap was never capital. It is navigation. *— §7, The Turn*

The programs exist. The receipts exist. What stalls applicants is the moat of process: merit criteria, content rules, offtake structuring, agency relationships. Washington funds those who know the building.

The clearest map comes from people who ran the building. Jonathan S. Hale led supply-chain diplomacy at the U.S. State Department, convened the first U.S.-led Supply Chain Ministerial, and drafted trade law for the Senate Commerce Committee. [J.S. Hale, “The Critical Minerals Race Is Heating Up,” Neil Hill Global Strategies, 30 March 2026]

## 8. The Close

### Canada waited for replacement capital. One replacement was written into U.S. law in 1992; the other two never needed it.

NPSI is watching the equity era: who takes Washington's next cheque, and who keeps waiting for Ottawa's.
