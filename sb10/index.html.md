# The 2027 Cliff

On 1 January the Pentagon stops contracting for magnets, tungsten and tantalum mined or refined in China. The rule is narrower than reported. The gap it exposes is wider.

A forensic assessment of United States legal, industrial, grid and workforce readiness for the defence critical-materials ban, with every claim in the circulating draft checked against the primary text.

## Summary

### Five findings

The cliff is real, but it is not where the draft put it. Magnet tonnage is not the constraint. The constraint sits upstream, in heavy rare earths, samarium and tungsten, where no contractor can fix supply in the 66 days left. [NPSI assessment]

300 t

Of about 48,000 tonnes of NdFeB magnets the United States used in 2025, about 300 came from domestic sources. Capacity is on track for 5,000 tonnes by the end of 2026. [Source: Arthur D. Little via Reuters (demand, supply); Reuters (capacity), 27 Jul 2026]

\>99%

China’s share of refined dysprosium and terbium, the heavy rare earths that keep a magnet working when a motor, actuator or seeker runs hot. [Source: Adamas Intelligence, Dec 2025]

16.3×

Dysprosium oxide outside China against the price inside it, late September. NdPr oxide, the light rare earth, now trades slightly below the Chinese price. [Source: Benchmark Mineral Intelligence, 25 Sep 2026]

0

Tungsten mines producing commercially in the United States, none since 2015. Tungsten APT rose from a 2025 low of $331 per unit to above $3,000 by April 2026. [Source: USGS, Feb 2026; Reuters, 29 Apr 2026]

Beijing says the arrangement under which it suspended its October 2025 rare earth controls now runs to 10 January, nine days after the rule takes effect. The suspension notice itself still reads 10 November. [Source: MOFCOM, 28 Sep 2026; MOFCOM Announcement 70, 7 Nov 2025]

## I · The rule

### Three instruments, three texts

**The statute.** 10 U.S.C. § 4872 bars the Department of Defense from acquiring covered materials melted or produced in a covered nation: China, Russia, Iran and North Korea. Covered materials are samarium-cobalt and neodymium-iron-boron magnets, tantalum metals and alloys, tungsten metal powder and tungsten heavy alloy. The FY2026 act added molybdenum at once, and gallium and germanium from 18 December 2027. [Source: Pub. L. 115-232 § 871, the FY2019 act; Pub. L. 119-60 § 844]

**The upstream step.** Section 844 of the FY2021 act would have reached back to material “mined, refined, separated” after five years. Section 854 of the FY2024 act changed five to six, which puts it on 1 January 2027. The FY2026 act then rewrote the instruction. From that date a new paragraph bars the Secretary from entering “into a contract for any covered material mined, refined, or separated in any covered nation.” [Source: Pub. L. 116-283 § 844; Pub. L. 118-31 § 854; Pub. L. 119-60 § 848]

**The regulation.** The DFARS clause in most contracts, 252.225-7052 of May 2024, carries the older wording, “mined, refined, separated, melted, or produced”, and turns on the delivery date. Clause 252.225-7961 (June 2026), issued under a class deviation revised on 1 July, turns on the award date, and it does not amend existing contracts by itself. A contract signed in 2025 with the older clause can still catch material delivered after 1 January; one law firm reads it as giving no general grandfather for inventory. [Source: 89 FR 46816, 30 May 2024; Pillsbury, 21 Oct 2026; Wiley, 24 Jul 2026]

**The exceptions narrow.** From 1 January a commercial off-the-shelf item escapes only if it is less than 50% covered material by weight; until now that test applied to tungsten alone. Mill products not yet in an end item, the bars, billets, slabs, plates and sheets, stay covered. The clause flows down to every tier, commercial items included. [Source: Pub. L. 116-283 § 844, as amended by Pub. L. 119-60 § 848; 48 CFR 225.7018-3]

Two clauses, one date, and a statute the regulation has not caught up with.

| Question | Until 31 December 2026 | From 1 January 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| Stages reached | Melted or produced | Also mined, refined or separated |
| COTS exception | Lost only if 50% or more tungsten by weight | Lost if 50% or more of any covered material |
| Item waiver | Up to 36 months where supply is unavailable at a reasonable price | Only with an accepted mitigation plan; national security waivers too, or on request to the NSA (EO 14415) |
| Covered metals | Magnets, tungsten, tantalum; molybdenum in statute | Same; gallium and germanium from 18 Dec 2027 |
| Clause trigger | 252.225-7052: delivery date | 252.225-7961: award date. Both may sit in one contract |

## II · The calendar

### Sixty-six days

The legal dates cluster inside a quarter. The supply that would answer them is spread across four years. Every arrival in the lower panel is a company target, and most sit after the date the rule takes effect.

Three dates in the first panel are not in the Pentagon’s control. Beijing’s notice suspending its October 2025 rare earth controls still expires on 10 November, though a September readout extended the wider trade arrangement to 10 January. Its pause on the December 2024 ban on gallium and germanium exports to the United States lapses on 27 November. No instrument extending either had been published at the time of writing. And the House’s FY2027 defence bill would repeal the upstream provision on the earlier of 31 December or enactment. [Source: MOFCOM Announcements 70 and 72, Nov 2025; MOFCOM, 28 Sep 2026; H.R. 8800 § 1803(c), 22 Jul 2026]

## III · The order

### The waiver becomes a plan

Executive Order 14415, *Securing America’s Defense Supply Chains and Ensuring Domestic Acquisition of Critical Materials*, was signed on 20 July and published three days later. [Source: 91 FR 46693, 23 Jul 2026]

Its centre of gravity is section 2. From 1 January the Secretary of War “shall cease to issue waivers” under § 4872(c)(1), the nonavailability exception. National security waivers under § 4872(e) follow the same terms, or need a request to the national security adviser. A waiver survives only where the Secretary accepts a contractor’s mitigation plan that documents exhaustive efforts and a timeline to phase out covered-nation material. Failure to qualify a domestic source “shall not constitute non-availability” unless the contractor shows “active, adequately funded, and ongoing efforts” to qualify one. [Source: EO 14415 §§ 2(a) to 2(c)]

Section 3 tells the rule-writers what to require: an indentured bill of materials traced to the origin of raw materials; vetting of suppliers for financial risk, foreign ownership, control or influence, and supply concentration; notice of significant risks within 15 days of vetting; and a confidential corrective action plan within 45. These bind no contractor until the regulations exist. Guidance is due around 16 January, the rules about 90 days later. [Source: EO 14415 § 3; Orrick, 1 Oct 2026]

The remedies are contractual: suspended task orders, declined options, termination, and referral to the Attorney General for fraud. The order does not mention the False Claims Act or debarment. That exposure appears in law-firm analysis, not in the text. [Source: EO 14415 §§ 2(d), 4; Orrick, 1 Oct 2026]

Section 6 leaves the government’s own channels alone: Project Vault, the $12 billion stockpile launched in February, and projects financed by EXIM or the DFC. In April, officials said Vault would first buy from “anywhere in the world,” China included. [Source: EO 14415 § 6; Reuters, 27 Jul 2026] The order tightens contractor sourcing and leaves the stockpile’s channel open. [NPSI assessment]

Congress may still move the floor. The House bill, passed on 22 July, rewrites § 4872 into a tiered regime and repeals the upstream bar on the earlier of 31 December or enactment. The Senate bill, reported but not passed, would push molybdenum to the gallium and germanium date. Neither is law, and the bill heads into a lame-duck session. [Source: H.R. 8800 § 1803; S. 4784 § 816; CRS IN12704]

#### What the order does

- Stops nonavailability waivers from 1 Jan, unless a mitigation plan is accepted
- Rules out “no domestic source qualified” as nonavailability
- Orders regulations on bills of materials, vetting and notice
- Exempts Project Vault, EXIM and DFC channels

#### What it does not say

- “Global” nonavailability
- False Claims Act or treble damages
- Debarment under FAR Subpart 9.4
- Any binding 15-day or 45-day clock before the rules exist

## V · The promoter’s read

### “One company is ready.” Its filings disagree.

On 25 October Oilprice.com asked whether anyone was ready for the deadline and answered that, for heavy rare earths in North America, “thus far appears to be one company: REalloys.” [Source: Oilprice.com, 25 Oct 2026] The disclosure at the foot says the site’s owner “owns shares and/or stock options of the featured company”, may sell them at any time, and that this “should be viewed as a major conflict with our ability to be unbiased.” It also says no one was paid to publish. The piece is one of a run of REalloys articles on the site, including a write-up of a Buy rating from Clear Street, the placement agent for the company’s June raise. [Source: Oilprice.com, Jul to Oct 2026; REalloys 10-Q, Q2 2026]

REalloys reached Nasdaq in February through a reverse merger with Blackboxstocks. In the second quarter it reported $0.8 million of revenue and a $36.8 million net loss, most of it share-based pay, and closed June with $122.4 million in cash after a $100 million placement. [Source: REalloys 8-K and 10-Q, 13 Aug 2026]

Its own filings set the dates; only samples are due before 1 January. [Source: REalloys 10-Q, Q2 2026; releases, 11 Mar and 29 Sep 2026]

| The narrative | What the filings say | On 1 January |
|---|---|---|
| Ready for 1 January | Qualification samples in Q4 2026, from recycled mixed oxide | Samples only |
| Saskatoon supply | 150 t NdPr metallization from Q1 2027; monazite to metal from Q3 2027 | Not yet |
| Heavy metal plant | About 50 t a year of Dy and Tb oxide feed; commissioning Q1 2028, slipped from early to mid 2027 | 12 to 15 months out |
| Magnets | JS Link letter of intent and Tooele lease, both non-binding | None |

The company has real assets, including a metals business in Euclid, Ohio that sells to the Defense Logistics Agency. What it does not have is compliant heavy rare earth metal at scale on 1 January. Nor does anyone else in North America. [NPSI assessment]

Three questions for any “ready for 2027” claim

**Where was the feed mined and separated?** From 1 January that is the test, not where the metal was made. **Is the offtake binding?** A letter of intent is not supply. **Is the product qualified?** Defence qualification takes years, and a sample is not a delivery.

## VI · The chain

### The grip is past the mine

The draft framed the cliff as a mining problem. The numbers put it further down the line.

China mined about 270,000 of the world’s 390,000 tonnes of rare earth oxide in 2025, or 69%. The United States mined 51,000. [Source: USGS, Feb 2026] But China separates and refines about 91% of magnet rare earths, makes about 94% of sintered magnets, and refines more than 99% of dysprosium and terbium. [Source: IEA, 23 Oct 2025; Adamas Intelligence, Dec 2025] The United States relied on imports for all of its heavy rare earths in 2021 to 2025, and USGS attributes 100% of its terbium imports in 2021 to 2024 to China. [Source: USGS, Feb 2026]

That is why the new verbs matter. “Melted or produced” caught the magnet maker. “Refined or separated” catches the chemistry in between, which is where the chain runs through China. [NPSI assessment]

## VII · The tonnes

### Defence is a sliver

The Pentagon has never published how many magnets it buys. The GAO puts its demand below 0.1% of world consumption. [Source: GAO-24-107176] One analyst estimates 200 to 300 tonnes of NdFeB and 200 to 400 tonnes of samarium-cobalt a year. [Source: InvestorNews, 7 Oct 2026] The stockpile plan for FY2025 listed 450 tonnes of NdFeB block as a potential purchase. [Source: USGS, Feb 2026]

American capacity already exceeds that. MP Materials began making magnets in Fort Worth in December 2025. eVAC’s plant in Sumter, South Carolina is rated near 2,000 tonnes, Noveon about 2,000, and USA Rare Earth targets a 600-tonne run rate in Oklahoma this quarter. [Source: MP Materials 10-K, 2025; eVAC; Noveon, 19 Jan 2026; USA Rare Earth, 10 Aug 2026]

The problem is who buys the rest. General Motors terminated its supply agreement with the Sumter plant on 1 July, which was to take about 90% of the output. eVAC is contesting the termination in arbitration. [Source: Reuters, 22 Oct 2026] Outside China, NdPr oxide traded at $95 a kilogram in August, under the $110 floor the Pentagon guarantees MP. [Source: Benchmark, 21 Aug 2026; MP Materials 8-K, Jul 2025]

The draft calls GM’s exit a relief valve: up to 1,800 tonnes a year freed for defence. The tonnes are real, and they exceed the whole estimated defence need. The feed is the catch. Sumter’s NdPr comes from Mountain Pass; its dysprosium and terbium must come from somewhere, and outside China that mostly means Lynas. A customer this small cannot fill the plants it depends on. [NPSI assessment]

## VIII · The heavy end

### The cheap part got built

A neodymium magnet loses strength as it heats. Dysprosium and terbium hold it in place, which is why they go into high-temperature grades for motors, actuators and guidance. Samarium-cobalt does the same job where heat is worse still.

China’s April 2025 controls on seven medium and heavy rare earths, samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium and yttrium, were never suspended. Exports need licences; some are now general. [Source: USGS, Feb 2026; China Countermeasures, 2026] In June, Beijing put MP Materials and USA Rare Earth on its export control list. [Source: Morgan Lewis, Jul 2026; Benchmark, Jun 2026]

The prices tell the rest. In September, NdPr oxide outside China cost $106.50 a kilogram against $108.70 inside it. Dysprosium oxide outside China averaged $3,500, and by late September stood at about 16.3 times the Chinese price, up from 6.8 times at the end of June. Terbium oxide outside China averaged $6,745. [Source: Benchmark, 25 Sep 2026] The Western build went after the volume element. The scarce ones are still priced in Beijing. [NPSI assessment]

### Nameplate is not output

Outside China, Lynas is the one commercial heavy rare earth separator on record. Lynas produced the first separated dysprosium outside China in Malaysia in May 2025, and terbium in June, and cites capacity of 240 tonnes of dysprosium and 50 of terbium a year, limited by feed. [Source: Lynas, Jul 2026] MP Materials is commissioning a 200-tonne circuit at Mountain Pass, with first output targeted for the end of 2026, for its own magnets. Energy Fuels plans 120 tonnes of dysprosium and 20 of terbium in Utah from late 2027, rising in a 2029 second phase. Aclara expects to finish building in Louisiana in late 2028, for an average of 200 and 35 over the project’s life. [Source: MP Materials, Nov 2025 and Sep 2026; Energy Fuels, 29 Jul 2026; Aclara, 13 Oct 2026]

Samarium is a step behind. Lynas made its first samarium oxide in March 2026; Energy Fuels plans 140 tonnes by the end of 2028. [Source: Critical Strategic Metals, 2026]

The industry has been here before. In September 2022 the Pentagon paused F-35 deliveries after finding Chinese-origin alloy in a Honeywell pump magnet, and resumed them in October under a national security waiver. [Source: Breaking Defense, Oct 2022] The widely quoted figure of 417 kilograms of rare earths per F-35 traces to an unreleased 2012 Pentagon study whose method has never been published. [Source: CRS R41744, 2013; Adamas Intelligence, 22 Apr 2026]

## IX · Beyond magnets

### Tungsten is the sharper edge

For tungsten, the mine-stage test arrives with nothing domestic behind it. China produced about 67,000 of the world’s 85,000 tonnes in 2025, and Beijing has licensed exports since February 2025. [Source: USGS, Feb 2026] Tungsten in a penetrator is spent when fired and is not recovered. Rotterdam APT passed $3,000 a unit in April, against a 2025 low of $331. [Source: Reuters, 23 Mar and 29 Apr 2026] In August, Commerce ordered all US tungsten scrap sold to US buyers for a year. [Source: Federal Register, 6 Aug 2026] In September the Department of War agreed to invest $450 million in Elmet through preferred equity and warrants, alongside a stockpile contract with a $2 billion ceiling and $150 million guaranteed. ## XI · The grid

### Time to power, not power

A magnet plant is not a smelter. Process data put NdFeB manufacturing at about 8.2 kWh of electricity per kilogram. [Source: NETL, 2014] A 10,000-tonne plant would draw about 9 megawatts on average; all 48,000 tonnes of US demand, refined from oxide up, roughly 80 to 275. [NPSI assessment] Its problem is the queue it joins. ERCOT, home to MP’s Fort Worth plant and its planned Northlake campus, had 474 GW of large loads asking to connect by June, 90% of them data centres. [Source: ERCOT, 29 Jul 2026] Power transformers averaged 128 weeks to deliver in 2025, and cost 77% more than in 2019. [Source: Wood Mackenzie via POWER, 2 Jan 2026]

Reliability bites first. Outages at Lynas’s Kalgoorlie plant were expected to cost about a month of production in the December 2025 quarter. [Source: Mining Weekly, 25 Nov 2025] USA Rare Earth’s filings warn that each of its sites depends on a single utility. [Source: USA Rare Earth 8-K, 2026]

Gallium and germanium are different. They are by-products of zinc smelters and alumina refineries. Korea Zinc’s Tennessee smelter assumes 1,849 GWh a year, about 211 MW. Century’s planned Oklahoma aluminium smelter still had no power contract after a year of talks. [Source: Korea Zinc, Jan 2026; Canary Media, 30 Apr 2026]

## XII · The people

### Plants are being built faster than engineers

The United States granted about 285 mining and mineral engineering degrees at all levels in 2024. The Department of Energy estimates about 6,000 new mining engineers are needed over the next decade, roughly 600 a year. Fourteen accredited programmes remain, from 25 in the early 1980s. [Source: MINING.COM, 25 Sep 2026] Reuters found at least 11 Chinese universities offering rare earth degrees, enrolling more than 500 students a year, and no undergraduate rare earth degree outside China. [Source: Reuters, 1 Jun 2026] “Anywhere else I need to train them for three years,” said Constantine Karayannopoulos, a veteran rare earth executive. [Source: Reuters, 1 Jun 2026]

Know-how is now litigated. MP Materials sued USA Rare Earth in May, alleging a former employee carried magnet process secrets across. [Source: Rare Earth Exchanges, 7 Jun 2026] The trades are contested too. Builders need 349,000 more workers this year and 456,000 in 2027, and the data centres in the same queue hire the same electricians. [Source: ABC, 15 Jan 2026; Fortune, 2 Mar 2026]

A new magnet plant takes two to four years to build and two to three more to qualify for defence. [Source: US Critical Materials, 14 May 2026, quoting an executive] The rule gives contractors 66 days. [NPSI assessment]

## XIII · Scenarios

### Three ways January goes

None of these is a forecast. Each names the signal that would show it is under way. [NPSI assessment]

A · Base case

#### The waiver becomes a plan

The rule takes effect. Programme offices accept mitigation plans for heavy-rare-earth magnets, samarium-cobalt and tungsten heavy alloy, each with a phase-out date. Deliveries slip at the margin, not wholesale. The paperwork moves from waiver to plan, and the material moves more slowly.

**Most exposed:** primes holding contracts with clause 7052 and deliveries due in early 2027.

Signal: national security determinations under § 4872(e), which must reach the defence committees before they are made, or within seven days in urgent cases.

B · Congress moves the floor

#### The lame duck rewrites § 4872

The FY2027 act adopts the House’s tiered regime or the Senate’s delay. The upstream bar is repealed before it bites, or reshaped. Contractors who spent on mapping keep the map; those who waited save the cost.

**Most exposed:** the domestic suppliers whose business case rests on the rule, from separators to magnet makers.

Signal: conference text of § 1803 or § 816 before 31 December.

C · Beijing moves first

#### The truce lapses on 10 January

The October 2025 measures return, including the rule reaching foreign-made goods with 0.1% Chinese rare earth content by value and the denial of licences for foreign military users. Compliant magnets made outside China with Chinese dysprosium become the next exposure.

**Most exposed:** allied magnet makers in Japan, Korea and Europe who still buy Chinese heavy rare earths.

Signal: a MOFCOM announcement on the suspension, or none, before 10 January.

Sources: 10 U.S.C. § 4872(e); H.R. 8800; S. 4784; MOFCOM Announcements 61, 70 and 72 of 2025 via CSET and Fastmarkets; MOFCOM, 28 Sep 2026.

I read the draft that started this the way I read anything that arrives fully formed. It had the right instinct and the wrong text. It credited the order with a “global” test it does not contain and a False Claims Act it never mentions, and it cited a statute that Congress rewrote ten months ago. The real rule is narrower, and that is not the comfort it sounds like.

Here is what I take from it. The West built the cheap part. Neodymium outside China now sells below the Chinese price. The expensive parts, dysprosium at sixteen times, tungsten at nine times its 2025 low, samarium barely started, are still Beijing’s to license. And the anchor customer of the Sumter magnet plant walked away in July.

Defence is a sliver of the market. It cannot carry these plants by itself, and it cannot buy around them any more without writing a plan that says when it will stop. On 2 January, somebody in a programme office will have to write that date down.

The rule sets a date. Supply chains do not read the Federal Register.

## Companion: Before the First of January

Companion brief · Contractor readiness

A working checklist for suppliers to the Department of War, drawn from NPSI Special Briefing NPSI-20261027, *The 2027 Cliff*. It reads public texts as of 27 October 2026. It is not legal advice, and a contracts lawyer should read your own clauses.

### Who is caught

- Any item, at any tier, containing a covered material: samarium-cobalt or neodymium-iron-boron magnets, tantalum metals and alloys, tungsten metal powder or tungsten heavy alloy. Commercial items included. [Source: 10 U.S.C. § 4872; DFARS 252.225-7052]
- From 1 January, material mined, refined or separated in China, Russia, Iran or North Korea, as well as melted or produced there. [Source: Pub. L. 119-60 § 848]
- Off-the-shelf items escape only if they are less than 50% covered material by weight. Mill products not yet in an end item stay covered. [Source: 48 CFR 225.7018-3]
- Molybdenum is in the statute but not yet in the clauses. Gallium and germanium join on 18 December 2027. [Source: Pub. L. 119-60 § 844]
- Prime purchases at or below the simplified acquisition threshold, and most electronic devices, remain excepted in the DFARS. [Source: 48 CFR 225.7018-3]

### Which clause is in your contract

| Clause | What triggers it | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| 252.225-7052 (May 2024) | Delivery date. The upstream bar applies to material delivered on or after 1 Jan 2027, even under older awards | Stock bought years ago can be caught. Seek an amendment |
| 252.225-7961 (June 2026) | Award date. The upstream bar applies to contracts awarded on or after 1 Jan 2027 | Map your bill of materials before you bid |
| Both in one contract | 7961 does not expressly displace 7052 | Ask for a written amendment deleting 7052 |

Clause readings follow Pillsbury, 21 Oct 2026, and Wiley, 24 Jul 2026. Class Deviation 2026-O0041 Rev. 1 itself could not be retrieved.

Ten actions

### What to do before 1 January

1. 01

   **Pull every open contract, subcontract and purchase order.** Record the clause number, award date and each delivery date. [Source: Pillsbury, 21 Oct 2026]
2. 02

   **Rank by exposure.** Deliveries due on or soon after 1 January under clause 7052 come first. [Source: Pillsbury, 21 Oct 2026]
3. 03

   **Ask for the amendment.** Where an award predates 2027, seek a written change deleting 7052 and inserting 7961. [Source: Pillsbury, 21 Oct 2026]
4. 04

   **Push it down the tiers.** A modification at the prime does not flow to your suppliers by itself. [Source: Pillsbury, 21 Oct 2026]
5. 05

   **Build the indentured bill of materials.** Trace each covered material to the mine of origin. The coming rules will require it. [Source: EO 14415 § 3(b)]
6. 06

   **Flag the hard parts first.** High-temperature NdFeB with dysprosium or terbium, samarium-cobalt, tungsten heavy alloy and powder, and tantalum powder processed in China. [NPSI assessment]
7. 07

   **Keep the paper.** Hold records of where each stage happened: mining, refining, separation, melting, production and final manufacture. [Source: Pillsbury, 21 Oct 2026]
8. 08

   **Draft the mitigation plan now.** Name the non-compliant source, document exhaustive efforts, set the removal steps and a timeline. Without one, no nonavailability waiver. [Source: EO 14415 § 2(b)]
9. 09

   **Fund the alternative.** Qualification efforts must be “active, adequately funded, and ongoing”. Failing to qualify a source is not, by itself, nonavailability. [Source: EO 14415 § 2(c)]
10. 10

    **Vet your suppliers.** Financial risk, foreign ownership or control, and supply concentration. Expect a 15-day risk notice and a 45-day corrective plan in the rules. [Source: EO 14415 § 3(b), (c)]

Do not assume

That the age of your inventory protects you under 7052; one law firm reads it as offering no general grandfather. That a waiver is routine; from 1 January it needs a plan. That Congress will repeal the bar; the House text would, but nothing is law. That China’s suspension dates are settled; the notices still read 10 and 27 November. [Source: Pillsbury, 21 Oct 2026; EO 14415; H.R. 8800 § 1803; MOFCOM, 2025 to 2026]
