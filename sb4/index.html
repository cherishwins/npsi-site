The Tollgate Markets

Where else does breaking the rules cost a fee instead of a future?

Abstract

On 17 September 2026, Turkey’s Capital Markets Board ordered 131 investment funds into liquidation in a single day. Inside them sat the savings of 455,758 people. In 2025 the same regulator had fined 297 people and companies a combined TRY 2.69 billion, about $65 to 70 million: roughly 1% of a single money market fund at the centre of the collapse. And under Article 107(3) of the Capital Markets Law, a person accused of transaction-based manipulation who pays the Treasury twice the benefit before an investigation opens faces no penalty at all.

The briefing gives such a place a name. A tollgate market is a market where breaking the rules is a toll you pay, not a wall you hit. It then asks how many other markets work the same way, and rates seventeen of them on enforcement in practice rather than on their statutes. Most write tough laws. The weakness lives in three places the statute books do not show: the gain nobody measures, the fine nobody collects, and the trial that never comes.

The arrests in Turkey are the loud part of the story; by 30 September prosecutors had named 217 suspects and jailed 56. No court has ruled on guilt in any of it, and nothing in the briefing is a finding of guilt. Its subject is the price list that came before the arrests.

42 / 5

The briefing’s bottom line: most markets write tough laws, and the tollgates open afterwards. When a regulator cannot prove the profit, a fine set as a multiple of the gain collapses to a flat amount; Vietnam’s fine for an unproven gain is VND 1.5bn, about $57,000. A fine imposed is not a fine collected. And where a payment can close a case, as in Turkey, the trial never comes.

The Ladder

Seventeen markets are rated from softest to strongest on four observed signals: the typical penalty against the typical gain, the reality of criminal prosecution, published collection rates, and whether a payment can close a case. Turkey before September 2026 sits at the soft end, with Vietnam, Bangladesh (rated provisionally and under review) and Indonesia; the United States sits at the strong end. “Very high” toll risk means the expected penalty sits clearly below the expected gain for a well-resourced manipulator.

The markets that most resemble pre-collapse Turkey today are Indonesia and Vietnam, with Thailand close behind and Bangladesh a provisional fourth. Severe sentences arrive after collapses: 40 years in Korea, 36 in Singapore, life in Indonesia. Fifteen further markets are listed as unrated; absence from the ladder is not clearance.

The Tollgate Test

Five signs that breaking the rules has a price in a market, and four numbers to ask its regulator for. Three or more signs, and the penalty code is the wrong document to read.

  1. Thin floats, fresh listings. Are recent listings tightly held, with a small share of stock that actually trades? Check: free float of the last 24 months of IPOs; index-provider float adjustments.
  2. Funds that own each other’s trades. Do fund managers, brokers and the companies they list sit in the same group? Check: underwriter, broker and fund manager under one roof.
  3. “Cash” that holds stock. Do money market or short-term funds hold heavy repo, and against what collateral? Check: repo share of assets; returns far above the policy rate.
  4. Promoters close to power. Do fund promoters or board members sit near government or market infrastructure? Check: board seats at depositories, exchanges, advisory posts.
  5. A price on prosecution. Can a payment or a settlement close a criminal case without a trial? Check: effective-repentance clauses; settlement share of sanctions.

Four numbers to demand

Collection rate: the share of imposed fines actually paid, by firms and by individuals.  ·  Median fine: not the statutory maximum, the middle fine actually imposed.  ·  Referrals and convictions: criminal referrals per year, and how many end in a verdict.  ·  Pay-to-close route: whether one exists, and how often it is used.

Method

Ratings judge enforcement in practice and are an assessment from the sourced data in the briefing, not a measurement; they are published so they can be checked. Evidence comes from regulator reports, court outcomes and press reporting, each dated in the source list. The briefing publishes its own weak points as open items, among them a fund-size range that runs from $16.5bn to more than $20bn depending on source and date, a provisional rating for Bangladesh, and figures for older episodes that were not re-verified.

Corrections

The first edition, v1.0 of 3 October 2026, circulated before this page was published. The release hosted here is v1.0.1 of 4 October, which corrects it after a check of its claims against their sources. The analysis and the ladder’s ratings are unchanged; the facts under them are corrected. Among the corrections: Article 107(3) removes the penalty only if payment comes before an investigation opens, while later payment reduces the sentence; an Indonesian supervisor described as sentenced was acquitted by the Supreme Court in 2022, and the line is withdrawn; Vietnam’s VND 1.5bn is the fixed fine when no gain is established, not a ceiling; Canada’s collection rates cover 2007 to 2012; and a Korean sentence is 20 years, not 15. The full list is on page 23 of the release.
