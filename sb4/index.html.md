# The Tollgate Markets

Where else does breaking the rules cost a fee instead of a future?

Turkey froze 131 funds holding the savings of 455,758 people. Its regulator’s fines for all of 2025 came to about 1% of a single one of them. This briefing gives such a place a name, a tollgate market, rates seventeen markets on enforcement in practice rather than on their statutes, and ends with a five-sign test anyone can run. The full text follows; the PDF, corrected on 4 October 2026, is the canonical release.

## I · The Day the Cash Froze

### They thought they were holding cash.

On 17 September 2026, Turkey’s Capital Markets Board ordered 131 investment funds into liquidation in a single day. Inside them sat the savings of 455,758 people. [SPK Bulletins 2026/60 and 2026/61, 17 Sep 2026 · MKK investor count, via Reuters]

Many of those savers owned money market funds, the product people treat like a bank account. One of the largest, Tera Portföy’s TP2, held about 63% of its assets in repo deals. Reuters reports that it returned 123% over a period when inflation ran at 60%, without giving the dates. [TEFAS fund data via yatirimci.ai, 16 Sep 2026 · Reuters explainer, 2 Oct 2026]

### Cash does not do that.

By 30 September, prosecutors had named 217 suspects and jailed 56. The justice minister had said the proceedings targeted people and structures alleged to have used “Ponzi-like methods.” No court has ruled on guilt in any of it, and nothing in this briefing is a finding of guilt. [Justice ministry figures via Reuters, 2 Oct 2026 · Justice minister’s statement, 18 Sep 2026]

### The arrests are the loud part of the story. The quiet part is the price list that came before them.

In 2025, Turkey’s regulator fined 297 people and companies a combined TRY 2.69 billion, about $65 to 70 million. That is roughly 1% of a single money market fund at the centre of the collapse. And under Article 107(3) of the Capital Markets Law, a person accused of transaction-based manipulation who pays the Treasury twice the benefit, at least TRY 500,000, before an investigation opens faces no penalty at all. The regulator’s 2026 complaints give 15 days to pay. [Legal500, 2026 · Capital Markets Law Art. 107(3) · SPK criminal-complaint bulletins, 2026]

A tollgate market is a market where breaking the rules is a toll you pay, not a wall you hit. *The term this briefing introduces*

This briefing asks how many other markets work the same way. The answer depends less on their laws than on three things their laws do not show.

## The Bottom Line

### Most markets write tough laws. The tollgates open afterwards.

Many markets in this study let regulators fine manipulators a multiple of the gain. The weakness lives in three places the statute books do not show.

#### 01

##### The gain nobody measures

When a regulator cannot prove the profit, multiples collapse to a flat amount. Vietnam’s fine for an unproven gain is VND 1.5bn, about $57,000.

#### 02

##### The fine nobody collects

From 2007 to 2012, Canada’s provincial regulators collected about 35.7% of penalties imposed. British Columbia collected 2.9%.

#### 03

##### The trial that never comes

Indonesia has 42 suspected market crimes under investigation and 5 final verdicts. In Turkey, an early payment means no penalty.

#### Where it matters now

The markets that most resemble pre-collapse Turkey today are Indonesia and Vietnam, with Thailand close behind and Bangladesh a provisional fourth. Severe sentences arrive after collapses: 40 years in Korea, 36 in Singapore, life in Indonesia.

#### What to do with it

Read a market’s collection rate before its penalty code. Screen any market with the Tollgate Test: five signs, four numbers. Treat Turkish fund values as unproven until liquidation recoveries are known, and watch MSCI’s November 2026 review.

## II · The Cash That Wasn’t

### How a savings fund came to lean on stocks that barely trade

On 21 August 2026, by the TEFAS data the analyst Hilmi Güvenal examined, Tera’s money market fund held TRY 203 billion. Sixty-two percent of it sat in reverse repo. [H. Güvenal, “The Devil Takes the Hindmost,” Substack, 18 Sep 2026]

A reverse repo is a short loan with a deposit attached. The fund hands over cash, the borrower hands over securities, and the deal unwinds days later. With government bonds as the deposit, it is about as dull as finance gets.

The trouble starts when the deposit is a share that barely trades. Investors call that a thin float: only a sliver of the company’s stock ever changes hands, so a few determined buyers can move the price. And the price is what everyone uses to value the fund.

That is the allegation at the centre of the Turkish case. Regulators and the press describe funds buying thinly traded Borsa Istanbul stocks and lifting their own valuations, with money market funds financing the chain through repo. The regulator has cited unsecured borrowing between related parties. [Reuters explainer, 2 Oct 2026 · SPK statements, Sep 2026 · Business Standard, 18 Sep 2026]

## IV · The Price List

### What manipulation cost in Turkey before the collapse

On paper, Turkish law is strict. Where a benefit is shown, administrative fines must be at least twice it, and companies can be fined up to the higher of 1% of revenue or 20% of pre-tax profit. In practice, the 2025 fine book looked like this. [Capital Markets Law Arts. 103–104; Legal500, 2026]

Fig. 07 · Drawn to scale: the fine book against what it policed. The bronze bars are a full year of fines, and the largest one in it.

Bloomberg reports that shares in Tera’s own brokerage rose almost 40,000% after their 2022 listing. In the same market, the regulator penalised 35 instances of order-based manipulation in 2025. [Bloomberg, 2026 · Legal500, 2026]

An administrative rule narrows one form of market abuse: misleading statements count only if the person also placed orders or traded around them. The criminal offence, Art. 107(2), requires that the person obtained a benefit. [SPK Communiqué VI-104.1, via Legal500, 2026 · Capital Markets Law Art. 107(2)]

### Article 107(3): the clause that turns a crime into a price

Transaction-based market fraud carries three to five years in prison. Then the law offers a way out, priced by timing. Under Article 107(3), a person who pays the Treasury twice the benefit, at least TRY 500,000, before an investigation opens faces no penalty. Paying during the investigation halves the sentence; paying at trial cuts it by a third. The regulator’s 2026 criminal-complaint bulletins give 15 days to pay. [Capital Markets Law Art. 107(1) and (3), mevzuat.gov.tr · SPK bulletins, 2026]

Even the September bulletins are complaints, not penalties, as Güvenal points out, and each one carries the same footnote about this effective-repentance route. [H. Güvenal, “Fonzide Ara Bilanço,” Substack, 2026]

## V · Paper Teeth

### Turkey’s statute was not the problem. Neither is anyone else’s.

Turkey, Korea, China, Thailand, Singapore and Australia all let fines reach multiples of the gain on paper. The tollgates open in three places the statute books do not show.

#### 01 · Measure

##### The gain nobody measures

A multiple needs a number to multiply. When the regulator cannot prove the profit, the fine falls back to a flat amount.

#### 02 · Collect

##### The fine nobody collects

A penalty ordered and never paid is a press release. Few regulators publish their collection rates.

#### 03 · Prosecute

##### The trial that never comes

Settlements, repentance clauses and slow courts turn crimes into negotiations.

### Exhibit 01: one chairman, two fines

[Figure: A grid of ten thousand squares, each one dong of the undisclosed sale; a ringed bronze sliver of fewer than two squares at the top left is the fine]

In 2017, Vietnam fined FLC Group chairman Trinh Van Quyet VND 65 million for an undisclosed sale of 57 million shares. The sale brought in about VND 400 billion. [Giáo dục, 14 Dec 2017 · Vietnam Investment Review]

In January 2022 the regulator fined him again, VND 1.5 billion, about $65,600 at the time, for an undisclosed sale of 74.8 million shares. It called that the heaviest penalty its rules allowed for the breach. [VietnamPlus · The Vietnamese, 2025]

Manipulation is fined at ten times the gain, but in several published cases the regulator recorded the gain as not determined. Then the multiple has nothing to multiply, and the fine is a fixed VND 1.5 billion. [Decree 156/2020, Art. 36, as amended · VietnamFinance]

### The fine nobody collects

Canada has no national securities regulator. It has 13 provincial and territorial commissions and one self-regulator, CIRO. They impose large penalties, mostly on small-cap fraudsters. The cheques are another matter.

[Figure: Horizontal bars, share of imposed penalties actually collected in Canada: CIRO from firms 94 to 100 percent; provincial regulators 35.7 percent 2007 to 2012; IIROC from individuals under 20 percent; CIRO from individuals 15 percent; BC Securities Commission 2.9 percent]

Fig. 10 · Share of imposed penalties actually collected, Canada. CBC analysis of 2007–12, via Case IQ; CIRO Enforcement Report 2023–24; Globe and Mail, 2017.

The Globe and Mail counted C$1.1 billion in unpaid fines in 2017. British Columbia’s commission is owed about C$430 million by more than 400 parties, collected C$12.7 million over six fiscal years, and expects about C$130 million never to come back. [Globe and Mail, 2017 · CBC, Mar 2024]

India shows the same gap at larger scale. SEBI lists ₹77,800 crore of dues as difficult to recover in its 2024–25 annual report, up from ₹76,293 crore a year earlier. More than ₹61,200 crore of that sits before court-appointed committees, much of it tied to two cases, Sahara and PACL. [SEBI annual reports via Business Standard, Aug 2024 and Aug 2025]

## VII · Seventeen Markets Up Close

### Where the toll is cheapest

Where the expected penalty sits furthest below the gain.

Rank 02 · Fails at: measure

#### Vietnam

VND 1.5bn floor

When no gain is established, a manipulation fine is fixed at VND 1.5bn. On appeal in June 2025, FLC’s chairman saw his three-year manipulation term removed, leaving a VND 4bn ($153k) fine on that count. His remaining seven-year sentence is for fraudulent appropriation, cut after more than VND 2.46 trillion in restitution.

[Decree 156/2020, as amended; Malay Mail, 26 Jun 2025; VietnamPlus]

Rank 04 · Fails at: prosecute

#### Indonesia

Rp240.65bn, 151 parties

OJK’s manipulation fines from 2022 to January 2026 average about Rp1.6bn (about $100,000) per party. OJK cited one case it had referred to prosecutors and 42 suspected criminal cases under investigation, 32 of them manipulation. Five concluded cases have final verdicts.

The big cases went through corruption courts instead: a life sentence for Benny Tjokrosaputro over Jiwasraya, and an order to repay about $370 million over Asabri.

[OJK, 9 Feb 2026, via Indonesia Business Post; Reuters; CNN Indonesia, 13 Jan 2023]

Rank 05 · Fails at: prosecute

#### Thailand

2,050 days

Cases settled through civil fines took 2,050 days on average, up from 519 in 2018. Some offenders paid fines of THB 500,000.

At Stark Corporation, at least THB 15.6bn of irregular transactions preceded a share collapse of more than 90%. The ex-chairman fled to the UAE, was returned in June 2024 and indicted; THB 2.54bn of his assets were seized.

[TDRI, Apr 2025; Nation Thailand; ACGA; Bloomberg, Feb 2024; Khaosod English, 23 Jun 2024]

Rank 03 · Provisional

#### Bangladesh

Under review

The Daily Star has reported that penalties below illegal gains kept manipulation profitable. In August 2025 it reported that fines now cover up to 90% of illicit gains, against about 20% before. The regulator has had to sue to collect unpaid fines.

The evidence now points both ways. Bangladesh stays on the ladder provisionally, its rating under review.

[Daily Star, 12 Aug 2025; TBS, 27 Oct 2024]

Real penalties on paper, and a gap at the cashier or the courtroom door.

Rank 06 · Fails at: collect

#### Canada

C$1.1bn unpaid

Thirteen commissions and no national regulator. CIRO collected 94–100% of fines from firms and 10–38% from individuals over FY22 to FY24.

In *Poonian* (2024), the Supreme Court of Canada held that bankruptcy wipes out administrative penalties, while disgorgement orders survive.

[CIRO Enforcement Reports; Globe and Mail, 2017; CBC, Mar 2024; 2024 SCC 28]

Rank 09 · Fails at: collect

#### India

807 of 3,871

Of SEBI’s recovery certificates, 807 of 3,871 were classed as difficult to recover by March 2024. Sahara and PACL account for ₹63,206 crore, 61.5% of the ₹1,02,830.75 crore due.

[SEBI FY24 annual report via Business Standard, Aug 2024]

Rank 07 · Fails at: prosecute

#### Brazil

R$832m, 186 people

The penalties the CVM imposed in 2023. Most cases end in settlements (*termos de compromisso*) with no admission of guilt.

One ABN Amro executive settled with the CVM for R$238,000; the criminal case against him was conditionally suspended. Scholars describe a severe penalty with a notoriously low chance of being enforced.

### Where the wall is real, and where it came late

Rank 10 · Moving up

#### South Korea

Since 19 Jan 2024

Until then, manipulation and insider trading could only be punished criminally, which the FSC says usually took a long time. The reform added surcharges up to twice the gain (KRW 4bn if the gain cannot be calculated), a statutory way to compute the gain, and 50–100% leniency for self-reporters. The first such surcharge came in September 2025. A later revision added bans of up to five years on trading and on serving as a listed-company executive.

Whether this narrows the “Korea discount” is not established here.

[FSC press releases; Kim & Chang]

Rank 11 · Heavy, and directed

#### China

RMB 15.5bn, 2025

661 penalty decisions and 172 criminal referrals in 2025, after 592 decisions and RMB 15.3bn in 2024.

It did not always deter. Tang Hanbo was fined RMB 39m in 2014 and RMB 15m in 2015 before a RMB 1.17bn order in 2017. Small fines first, then escalation once they failed.

[Caixin, 20 Apr 2026; Caixin, 2017; Bloomberg, Mar 2017]

#### Ranks 12–17 · The strong end

**United States.** 456 actions in FY2025; routine parallel criminal cases; a 20-year maximum. [SEC, 7 Apr 2026]
**United Kingdom.** Insider dealing maximum rose to 10 years on 1 Nov 2021, from 7. [FCA]
**EU under MAR.** Sets minimum maximums, not caps: at least €5m for people, €15m or 15% of turnover for firms. [Regulation 596/2014]
**Australia.** Raised the prison maximum to 15 years in 2019 and added civil penalties of up to three times the benefit. [ASIC]
**Hong Kong.** Record manipulation sentence of 6 years 8 months in 2024, still under the 10-year cap. [Sidley, Jul 2024]
**Singapore.** 36 years for the 2013 penny-stock crash that erased more than S$8bn, upheld in March 2026. [Malay Mail, 18 Mar 2026]

Even at the strong end, what is ordered and what is paid are not the same number. The difference is in how fast the wall goes up.

## VIII · After the Damage

### The harshest sentences arrive after the money is gone

Prison terms in fund and manipulation scandals can be enormous. Look at when they land.

[Figure: Horizontal bars, years of prison handed down after collapses: Benny Tjokrosaputro life; Kim Jae-hyun 40 years; Soh 36; Kim Bong-hyun 30; Quah 20; Lee Jong-pil 20; Trinh Van Quyet 7]

Optimus’s chief executive raised more than KRW 1.3 trillion with promises of public-sector-backed assets. Kim Bong-hyun, the Lime scandal’s central figure, drew 30 years. In Indonesia, prosecutors had sought death for Benny Tjokrosaputro. [Korea Herald; Asia Business Daily; Jakarta Post]

Before the collapses, the same markets moved slowly. Korea could only punish manipulation through criminal courts until 2024, which its regulator says usually took a long time. Even after Jiwasraya and Asabri, Indonesia’s OJK fines from 2022 to January 2026 averaged about $100,000 a party. [FSC; OJK, 9 Feb 2026]

A forty-year sentence tells the next manipulator what happens after a collapse. It says little about the years before it, when the fines were small and the funds were growing. *NPSI assessment, drawn from the cases above*

## IX · The Cash That Wasn’t, Elsewhere

### Turkey’s savers were not the first to be sold liquidity that wasn’t there

The Turkish pattern has a family tree: products sold as safe or redeemable that sat on assets that could not be sold at the price on the statement, and positions so concentrated that the price was whatever their owner paid.

Korea · 2019–20

#### Lime

KRW 1.6 trillion suspended

The FSS found Lime sold open-ended funds, redeemable at any time, while holding illiquid assets. Leverage came through about KRW 670bn of total return swaps with three brokers.

[FSS findings, via Korea Herald]

Korea · 2020

#### Optimus

KRW 1.3 trillion raised

Marketed as low-risk public-sector receivables. The money went into risky assets or paid earlier investors. Its CEO’s 40-year term was finalised in July 2022.

[Korea Herald; Korea Times, Jul 2022]

United States · 2021

#### Archegos

Concentration behind swaps

Bill Hwang built concentrated positions in a few names through swaps that kept them off public disclosure. The same own-the-float dynamic, at prime-broker scale. He was convicted of fraud in 2024 and sentenced to 18 years.

[US v. Hwang, SDNY, 2024]

India · 2018 and 2020

#### IL&FS, Franklin Templeton

Debt funds closed

Short-term and credit funds turned out to hold illiquid paper. Detailed figures were not re-verified for this briefing.

China’s wealth management products and trust defaults, where savers treated bank-sold products as deposits. Bernie Cornfeld’s Investors Overseas Services, the 1960s fund of funds that became the archetype of self-dealing. Neither re-verified here.

Money market funds with more than 60% in repo, financing related parties and illiquid shares. The SPK cites unsecured related-party borrowing. Interim payouts are to begin with the Tera, Pusula, Atlas and Hedef funds.

## X · Who Looks Like Turkey Now

### Four markets share the pre-collapse profile

None of them is Turkey. Each shows enough of the same signs to deserve the same questions.

1. #### Indonesia Closest match

   An index-provider warning: MSCI froze Indonesian index changes in January 2026 and warned in June that it may consult on moving Indonesia to Frontier status if progress is not evident by November. Widely reported “fried stock” (*saham gorengan*) pump-and-dumps. Per-party fines around $100,000. A precedent of institutional money pushed into inflated, illiquid stocks at Jiwasraya and Asabri.
2. #### Vietnam Close

   A fixed fine of about $57,000 whenever no gain is established, a retail-dominated market, and a manipulation term dropped on appeal in the FLC case.
3. #### Thailand Close

   A low fallback fine when gains cannot be measured, enforcement lags measured in years, and a major accounting fraud whose chairman fled abroad before being returned to face charges.
4. #### Bangladesh Provisional

   Press reports of penalties below gains and repeat offenders, now set against a 2025 reform that lifted fines toward the gains. Under review.

#### The Turkish signature, in five parts

1 Concentrated, low-float listings · 2 Related-party fund chains · 3 “Cash” products holding repo against those shares · 4 Promoters close to political power · 5 A legal route that puts a price on the crime

The test below turns these five parts into a test anyone can run on any market, with four numbers to demand from its regulator.

## The Tollgate Test

### Five signs that breaking the rules has a price in a market, and four numbers to ask its regulator for.

Three or more signs, and the penalty code is the wrong document to read.

1. #### Thin floats, fresh listings

   Are recent listings tightly held, with a small share of stock that actually trades?

   Check: free float of the last 24 months of IPOs; index-provider float adjustments.
2. #### Funds that own each other’s trades

   Do fund managers, brokers and the companies they list sit in the same group?

   Check: underwriter, broker and fund manager under one roof.
3. #### “Cash” that holds stock

   Do money market or short-term funds hold heavy repo, and against what collateral?

   Check: repo share of assets; returns far above the policy rate.
4. #### Promoters close to power

   Do fund promoters or board members sit near government or market infrastructure?

   Check: board seats at depositories, exchanges, advisory posts.
5. #### A price on prosecution

   Can a payment or a settlement close a criminal case without a trial?

   Check: effective-repentance clauses; settlement share of sanctions.

#### Four numbers to demand

**Collection rate.** Share of imposed fines actually paid, by firms and by individuals.

**Median fine.** Not the statutory maximum. The middle fine actually imposed.

**Referrals and convictions.** Criminal referrals per year, and how many end in a verdict.

**Pay-to-close route.** Whether one exists, and how often it is used.

## From the Author

Signed editorial · First-person register

### The price list

I came to this through Patrick Boyle’s video on the Tera collapse. His framing was simple: weak institutions and tiny fines let bad actors drive out honest businesses. I wanted to know where else that was true, so I went looking for the fine that is smaller than the profit.

I expected to find weak laws. I didn’t. Turkey, Korea, China, Thailand, Singapore and Australia all let fines run to multiples of the gain. On paper, the lawmakers did their jobs.

What I found was everything that happens after the law. Vietnam’s regulator often recorded no measurable profit, so the fine fell to a fixed fee. Canada’s regulators collected about a third of what they imposed between 2007 and 2012, and British Columbia, where NPSI is based, collected 2.9%. Turkey wrote the exit into the statute itself: pay twice the benefit before an investigation opens and no penalty follows.

Then the sentences. Forty years in Korea. Thirty-six in Singapore. Life in Indonesia. Every one of them handed down after the money was gone.

#### My view, and mine alone

A penalty nobody collects is a toll, and a toll that costs less than the gain is a business model.

The people who lost money in Turkey were not careless. They bought the product their market sold them as cash.

I would read a market’s collection rate before I read its penalty code. The Tollgate Test above is how I now screen one: five signs, four numbers. Use it, argue with it, sharpen it.

Turkey did not lack laws. It had a price list. Every market has one somewhere. The tollgate markets are the ones where somebody read it before the regulator did.

J.J. · Jesse James · Founding Editor, NPSI · Victoria, British Columbia
