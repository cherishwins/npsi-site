One Day, Four Names

What Tokyo, Seoul, Pyongyang and Washington each decided to call 15 August 1945. And the subject every name leaves out.

The PDF below is the canonical release: twenty-nine pages, in a dark edition for distribution and a cream edition for print, corrected on 4 October 2026 after a check of its claims against their sources, with the corrections listed on its page 28. Abstract

The war in the Pacific ended once. It has at least six official names, and none of them is neutral. Each name is a sentence with a subject removed, and where the subject went is where the policy lives. Japan’s official name for 15 August, fixed by cabinet decision on 13 April 1982, is the Day for Mourning the War Dead and Praying for Peace: it mentions neither the war’s end nor its loss. Seoul’s name says the light was restored without naming who restored it. Washington’s says victory over Japan, and Korea is not in the sentence.

The briefing reads the names as primary sources against the documents of 1945. On 16 August Stalin accepted the order that split Korea without mentioning Korea, and in the same message asked for the northern half of Hokkaido. Truman declined. Japan stayed whole; Korea was divided. The familiar story of two colonels, thirty minutes and a National Geographic map turns out to merge three documents carrying two different dates. The briefing then reads the speeches of 15 August 2026 against the names, and finds that each did what its name predicted. The grammar fixed in 1945, 1949, 1963 and 1982 still predicts the quarrels of August 2026.

The Four Names

終戦の日

Tokyo · “the war ended.” The common name. The official one, by cabinet decision of 1982, is the Day for Mourning the War Dead and Praying for Peace.

광복절

Seoul · Gwangbokjeol, “the light was restored.” Statute of 1 October 1949; it marks both liberation in 1945 and the founding of the government in 1948.

조국해방의 날

Pyongyang · “the fatherland was liberated.” The official history gives the act to Kim Il-sung’s guerrillas, and since 2024 to Soviet soldiers too.

V-J Day

Washington · “victory over Japan.” Proclaimed for 2 September 1945 in Truman’s radio address of 1 September.

Moscow’s name, set by federal law in June 2023, and Beijing’s, made statutory by the National People’s Congress Standing Committee in February 2014, complete the six. None of the six says who drew the line across Korea.

Key Findings

  1. Japan’s official name for the day mentions neither the war’s end nor its loss. [Cabinet decision, 1982]
  2. On 16 August 1945 Stalin accepted the order that split Korea without mentioning Korea. In the same message he asked for the northern half of Hokkaido, and Truman declined. [FRUS 1945 VI, docs 450, 452]
  3. The “two colonels, thirty minutes, a National Geographic map” story merges three documents carrying two different dates. Dean Rusk’s own account in the government record has no stopwatch and no map. [FRUS doc 771; Schnabel; Rusk]
  4. Kim Il-sung reached Korea on 19 September 1945, five weeks after liberation. In 2024, 2025 and 2026 his grandson marked the anniversary by laying wreaths for Soviet soldiers. [Rodong Sinmun, via NK News]
  5. On 15 August 2026 Japan’s Emperor spoke of “deep remorse.” The Prime Minister did not use the word. A year earlier her predecessor had put it back after twelve years without it. [Kantei, 2012–2026; Kyodo]

The reading rule

01  Read the official name of the day before the speech.  ·  02  Ask which subject it removes: who acted, who lost, who is not in the sentence.  ·  03  Read the speech. See whether the subject came back.

Method

Dated claims trace to named documents, among them the cables of August 1945 in Foreign Relations of the United States. Where a widely repeated claim rested on a single or erroneous origin, the chain is shown rather than the claim repeated. Germany is the control case: Richard von Weizsäcker’s address of 8 May 1985 held both defeat and liberation in one sentence. The briefing publishes its open items, among them Rusk’s two dates for the night the line was drawn and a pending native-speaker review of its Korean and Japanese lines.

Claims this briefing does not make

That Kim Il-sung “won the Second World War”: the DPRK claims the leading role in Korea’s liberation, not the war. That Japan “rewrote invasion as advance” in 1982 as a blanket fact, or that the episode was a hoax. That the 38th parallel was drawn on a 1904 map, or in thirty minutes by both colonels. That the Prime Minister “skipped” Yasukuni in 2026.

Corrections

The first edition, v1.0 of 3 October 2026, is superseded by the corrected v1.0.1 of 4 October hosted here, issued after a check of its claims against their sources. The argument is unchanged; quotations, dates and two figures are corrected. Among the corrections: Stalin’s 16 August sentence is now quoted whole, with its condition on the Liaotung Peninsula; a line attributed to President Putin is Reuters’ paraphrase, not a quotation; President Lee proposed talks among the two Koreas and the United States, or those three and China; “remorse” appears in two of fifteen 15 August addresses, 2012 and 2025; and the figure tracing the 38th-parallel legend now names the document behind each clause. Two sentences about Washington are softened. The full list is on page 28 of the release.
